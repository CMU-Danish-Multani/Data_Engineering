STOCK INTELLIGENCE FOR AI INFRASTRUCTURE AND BANKING

A data pipeline and dashboard that compares companies within their own industry

TeamMembersProject managerMeeting notes
Team 8Anav Batra, Akshay Iyer, Mohd Danish Multani, Alan ZhouAnav BatraTeam Google Doc
Summary We will build a Stock Intelligence Dashboard that enables retail investors to compare publicly listed AI infrastructure companies and banks using industry-specific financial metrics, institutional investment activity, and management commentary. A data pipeline will integrate nine public datasets from seven providers, covering structured tables, semi-structured JSON and unstructured text, and store each in the system that fits its shape: PostgreSQL, MongoDB or Amazon S3. By centralizing this information, the dashboard will help retail investors stay informed about financial performance, track changes in company fundamentals, compare industry peers, check whether management's words match the numbers, and identify potential investment opportunities.

Background and Problem Statement

Retail investors often struggle to stay up to date with the financial performance of companies across industries. Companies regularly publish financial information through SEC filings, earnings reports and other public sources, but this information is scattered across different platforms, reported in different formats and updated at different intervals. For example, companies like Microsoft, Google, Meta and Amazon are investing billions of dollars in AI infrastructure, and investors need to track capital expenditure, revenue growth, operating margins and free cash flow to understand whether these investments are accompanied by improving financial performance. Similarly, investors evaluating banks need to monitor loan growth, net interest margins and loan losses to understand their financial health as interest rates change. The relevant information also sits outside filings, in regulator databases, economic statistics, earnings calls and consumer reports. Although all of it is public, investors must gather and compare it by hand, which makes it time-consuming to track changes in fundamentals, compare companies within an industry, and identify improving or deteriorating performance.

Problem statement. How can retail investors compare companies using financial performance, institutional investment activity, and qualitative information?

Project Goal

Our goal is to build a Stock Intelligence Dashboard that consolidates key financial metrics across two industries, AI infrastructure and banking. For AI infrastructure it starts with five companies, Microsoft, Alphabet (Google), Amazon, Meta and NVIDIA, tracked at both quarterly and annual frequency; for banks it tracks loan growth, net interest margin and loan losses. It will compare each company against its industry peers, show how performance changes over time, and show how major institutional investors' reported holdings are shifting.

Key Metrics by Industry

Each industry is judged on different figures, so the dashboard uses a separate set of key performance indicators (KPIs) for each, plus a shared set that applies to both. For AI infrastructure we will track ten KPIs per company. Seven are standardized financial KPIs that allow a like for like comparison across all five companies. Three are company-specific product KPIs that reflect each company's core business and how it makes money, such as Azure revenue growth (Microsoft), AWS revenue growth (Amazon), advertising revenue growth (Meta) and Data Center revenue growth (NVIDIA); these explain the business drivers behind the financial results.

Product KPIs need a different route from the standardized ones. The SEC company facts API carries only whole-company totals, not breakdowns by segment or product, so product figures will be extracted from the segment notes in the filings and from 8-K earnings releases. Some are reported only as a growth percentage rather than a dollar amount; Microsoft, for example, mainly reports Azure as a percentage growth rate.

Research Questions

The questions below are examples of what the integrated data can answer; the dashboard is designed to support similar questions as the project develops.

Question 1. How have fundamentals changed over the past five years? Which AI infrastructure companies show the strongest revenue growth, operating margins and free cash flow relative to capital expenditure, and does their spending track national data center construction? Which banks grew loans fastest without rising loan losses, how did the 2022 to 2023 rate increases change their net interest margins, and did these leaders outperform their peers on stock return?

Question 2. Where is institutional money moving? Which companies saw the largest increases or decreases in holdings among the largest hedge funds, ranked by 13F portfolio value, over the past four quarters, and are institutions adding to companies with improving fundamentals?

Question 3. Do words match the numbers? Does management discussion of AI investment, in filings and on earnings calls, track actual capital expenditure and free cash flow? For banks, do credit risk language in filings and the volume and themes of consumer complaints rise before loan losses do?

How the Data Stays Up to Date

None of these sources publishes data continuously: they update nightly, daily, monthly or quarterly, as Table 3 shows. Streaming would therefore add cost and complexity without making the data any fresher, so we will use scheduled batch loading instead. The pipeline starts with a one-time historical backfill for fiscal years 2019 to 2025. After that, a scheduler (cron, or Apache Airflow if time allows) runs one job per source on that source's own cadence, and each job requests only records newer than the last successful run. No manual downloads are needed once the jobs are set up.

Every run writes its raw files to a new dated folder or batch, logs how many records it loaded, and is safe to repeat: running the same job twice does not create duplicates. If a download fails or returns an empty or malformed file, the job stops before writing and is retried on the next run, so a bad batch never reaches the cleaned tables.

Why each storage system. PostgreSQL holds the flat, tabular sources because they load directly into tables and the course requires structured data there; every raw column is stored as text with no constraints, so no problematic record is rejected at load time. MongoDB holds FDIC and CFPB records because each is a self-contained document whose fields vary from record to record, and complaint narratives are long free text. Amazon S3 holds the SEC bulk files, filing text and call transcripts because they are large, have no fixed schema, and are written once and read in batches. Figure 1 shows the full flow.

Processing: Exploration, Cleaning and Integration

Every source is profiled on arrival: row counts, missing values, duplicates, value ranges and units. Cleaning runs in SQL for PostgreSQL data and in aggregation pipelines for MongoDB data, and every quality issue is logged with its decision. The hardest step is integration: the same company appears as a CIK number in SEC filings, a ticker in prices and call transcripts, a CUSIP code in 13F holdings, a certificate number at the FDIC and a free-text name in consumer complaints.

Significance and Beneficiaries

Retail investors gain the most: one place to compare companies on the metrics each industry is judged by, built only from public data, without manually researching multiple sources. Students, investment clubs and financial journalists gain the same free, traceable view. The first question shows whether the AI build-out is generating returns and which banks are lending safely, the second shows whether professional money agrees, and the third flags companies whose narrative and numbers diverge, an early warning sign worth checking before investing.

Team Motivation

We chose this problem because of our interest in financial markets and the opportunity to apply data engineering to a practical challenge faced by retail investors. It exercises every stage of the course on real data of three different types, stored in three different systems and joined across five kinds of company identifier.

Future Scope

Once the two-industry version works, we plan to add more industries through the same pipeline by adding each industry's metrics to a shared metric dictionary rather than writing new code. We also plan a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) assistant that answers questions like the ones above in plain language: it would retrieve the relevant figures from the cleaned tables and passages from filings, call transcripts and complaints, reason over them, and cite its sources, so investors no longer have to check each detail by hand.
